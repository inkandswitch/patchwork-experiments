# The 2011 surge: a natural experiment

In 2011 the number of first-year students in Aachen jumped by more than a quarter, for reasons that had nothing to do with housing. Conscription ended, NRW abolished tuition fees, and Bavaria and Lower Saxony sent two years of school leavers at once; NRW followed in 2013. Because housing didn't cause the surge, it is the cleanest test of what students do to rents. In the model the surge raised rents by 4% by 2015 and 8% by 2020: about a quarter to a third of the real rise. That is more than other studies suggest.

## The surge

First-year numbers went from 7,100 in 2010 to 9,000 in 2011 and 9,700 in 2013, and they never fell back: 9,300 students started in 2025. The comparison run keeps them at their 2010 level, so every cohort since is smaller by the difference. By 2020 that adds up to about 10,700 fewer students.

Only the first few years are a clean experiment. Later, first-year numbers probably stayed high for other reasons too, such as more school leavers going to university and more international students.

| Model | +2.1% | +4.4% | +7.7% | +8.5% |

Real asking rents rose 17% from 2010 to 2015 and 26% to 2020, in 2000 prices. The surge explains about a quarter of the first and nearly a third of the second.

We expected the gap to close once the extra cohorts graduated, around 2020. It keeps widening instead, because first-year numbers never returned to their 2010 level.

## Compared with other studies

Nobody has measured this shock's effect on German rents directly. The nearest estimates come from 68 German cities between 2004 and 2013, and from international students in US college towns. Scaled to Aachen, they imply 0.06% to 0.35% higher rent per 1,000 extra students. The model gives 0.46% per 1,000 by 2013 and 0.72% by 2020: just above that range at first, then two to more than ten times higher.

Two things suggest the model is too strong. With 2.5 students to a flat instead of 1.8, the effect by 2020 drops to 0.55% per 1,000. And in the model high rents never keep students away, while a German study finds they deter first-years who would move from far away.

The real data is also less clear than the model. Rents rose across Germany in the same years. And in Aachen, asking rents for one-room flats, the students' segment, rose 15% from 2010 to 2013, hardly more than for three- and four-room flats (11% and 12%).

# Switching loops off

Of the five feedback loops, only one shapes the results: "Priced out" (B2). Switch it off and rents run away under every condition we tried. The builders' forecast of growth is the other piece the model can't do without. Without it, the model builds only about half of what Aachen actually built. The other four loops barely matter, even under extreme conditions, because building land caps building in almost every year. Anything else that pushes building up has no room to act.

## The protocol

Sterman calls this a behaviour anomaly test:

> Behavior anomaly tests examine the importance of these structures by asking whether anomalous behavior arises when the relationship is deleted or modified. Anomalous behavior generated by deletion of a relationship provides you with some evidence for its importance. *(Sterman, Business Dynamics, p. 880)*

He also says how to delete a loop:

> In loop knockout tests you zero out a target relationship. … You can also eliminate loops by setting the delay times in information delays to infinity and by setting nonlinear functions y = f(x) to unity for all values of x. *(p. 880)*

In this model each loop's effect is raised to its loop strength, so a strength of 0 makes the effect 1: the second method. For "Political response" (B4) it sets subsidised building to zero, and the builders' forecast is switched off with GROWTH_RESPONSE = 0. "Pipeline awareness" (B5) is off by default, so the test switches it on.

Running the knockouts on the recorded history alone isn't enough:

> Loop knockout analysis can be revealing when the model is operating under historical conditions but is particularly effective in conjunction with extreme condition tests. Often a loop is not active under normal operating conditions but becomes dominant in unusual circumstances. *(p. 881)*

So every knockout runs under four conditions: the recorded run and three extremes. In the **student flood**, three times the usual number of new students arrive each year from 2012 to 2016, so the student population doubles to 71,000. In **no new land**, the city stops designating building land in 2012. In **no new students**, the universities stop admitting anyone in 2012.

Sterman also asks for written expectations, because a surprise is information:

> Discrepancies between model behavior and expectation indicate that there are flaws in the formal model, the mental model, or both. *(p. 882)*

The expectations for the extreme runs were written on 28 October 2026, before running them, and are kept with each scenario. The knockouts on the recorded run had already been tried earlier that day, so they weren't a blind test.

## "Priced out" is essential

```chart

scenarios: real, real without spillover
series: rent
years: 2000–2025
```

Without B2, nobody gives up a flat or moves away when rent rises. Rent climbs as long as there is any shortage: €31 by 2025 in the recorded run, with about 7,800 households left with no flat at all. In the student flood, rent keeps rising until 2030, fourteen years after student numbers peaked, while the students are already leaving. Without new students it drifts down to the €3 floor with one flat in ten empty, because no one moves into the empty flats.

Sterman's rule for this case:

> If a loop knockout test generates bizarre or physically impossible behavior under extreme conditions you have evidence that the relationship is important and must be included, even if it is not normally active and cannot be estimated statistically from the data. *(p. 881)*

B2 is also where the biggest uncertainty sits: how strongly households react to rent (see "How much of the rent rise is students"). The knockout sets a floor for it: any value near zero gives runaway rents.

## Four loops that do almost nothing

Switching off building for profit (B1), land prices (B3) or politics (B4), or switching on pipeline awareness (B5), moves rent by less than 20 cents in any year of any condition. The reasons:

- **Land caps building almost all the time.** The city designates land for about 560 flats a year, the average actually built. In 90% of the quarters from 2005 to 2024, builders want to build more than that. While the cap holds, more profit (B1) or subsidies (B4) can't add a single flat. Subsidies reach 228 flats a year at the height of the flood, but they only use land market builders would have used.
- **Land works through the cap, not its price.** Land only gets dearer (B3) once the reserve drops below about 1,800 flats' worth, and by then the cap already binds.
- **B5 only adjusts replacement building**, 121 flats a year. Building for expected growth doesn't look at the pipeline.

The cap comes from a small land reserve: 15 years of replacing old flats, about three years of real building. A test run with a reserve of 15 years of real building removed the cap. But then the model built 714 flats a year instead of 562, and rent rose only 10% from 2010 to 2025, against 27% in the data. So the small reserve stays, and with it the cap.

## Expectations against results

- **Student flood:** we expected the surplus after the flood to come from overbuilding. But flats grew by only 1,200, because building land ran low within five years and capped building. The surplus (vacancy up to 6.0%) comes from students leaving faster than priced-out households return. Rent ends at €5.98, back where it started rather than clearly below.
- **Student flood without builders' forecast:** we expected a much higher peak. It is only 11 cents higher, again because land, not builders, sets the limit.
- **No new land without B3:** we expected the land to run out sooner. It makes no visible difference.
- **No new students without B1:** we expected no change. Rent ends 9 cents lower, because builders keep building at the normal rate into a glut.
- **No new land:** we expected building to stop within a few years, and it did: starts fall from 494 a year in 2012 to 33 in 2020. Rent keeps climbing to €10.10 by 2040, as expected.
- **Rent rising while students leave** (flood without B2) wasn't expected at all. It shows that without B2, any shortage pushes rent up forever.

## Removing the delays

Sterman describes a second kind of anomaly test:

> Another form of behavior anomaly test replaces a disequilibrium structure with a simplified structure that assumes a subsystem is in equilibrium. For example, perception and material delays can be eliminated, in effect assuming decision makers can instantly recognize changes in the states of the system and instantly alter those states. *(p. 881)*

This run cuts building to six months and moving out, political recognition and builders spotting growth to three months each.

```chart
title: The recorded run with and without delays
scenarios: real, real-instant
series: rent
years: 2000–2040
```

We expected lower, smoother rents. Up to 2025 the two runs are almost the same (€7.51 against €7.53). After 2025 rent stays higher, €7.27 against €7.10 in 2040. The delay that matters is builders' forecasting: with a three-year delay they keep building for growth that has already slowed, and those extra flats ease rents later. So the delays don't change the history, but they do change the forecast.

## The other tests in Sterman's table

Table 21-4 (pp. 859–861) lists twelve tests. Three more are quick to run here:

- **Integration error** ("Cut the time step in half and test for changes in behavior", p. 860): the recorded run is unchanged at half the time step, and so is the run without delays.
- **Extreme conditions:** the balanced run stays flat for 40 years. With no new students, flats empty out and rent falls to about €4.40.
- **Behaviour reproduction:** compared with real asking rents in "How much of the rent rise is students".
